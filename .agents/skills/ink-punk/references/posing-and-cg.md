# Pose, acting and visual-novel CG direction

Keep the output branches distinct: a dialogue sprite or standalone character
remains on genuine transparency, even with an expressive pose. A CG combines
the character and a background into the story illustration. Camera drama alone
does not authorize adding scenery to a requested transparent character asset.

Use for expressive character art and gallery CGs. Keep the existing Ink Punk
identity and rendering canon. This branch adds acting, body mechanics and
camera;
it is not another costume/style generator. Poses can be quiet, awkward,
confident,
funny, intimate or dynamic according to the scene. A tilted hip is not the
only
available emotional language.

## Begin with a beat

Before choosing limbs or lens, complete a compact statement:

> In [situation], this character [specific action] toward [viewer/other
> actor/object],
> feeling [emotion], while [contrasting attitude or physical circumstance].

For example, an adult punk heroine who has just won an arcade match leans
toward
the player with a teasing wink; a normally confident companion hesitates
before
taking an offered hand; someone sits on the floor laughing after a mishap.
Use the requested story rather than inventing unrequested relationships or
events.
Reuse an established beat; do not force a questionnaire for a simple pose
edit.

Differentiate the deliverable: a standing sprite needs reusable clarity; a
fashion
portrait shows a design; a gallery CG captures a memorable moment. A CG may
crop
the body or emphasize a gesture. It need not show both shoes or display every
costume detail. Do not add sprite grids, UI or dialogue boxes unless
requested.

## Construct the pose as a whole

Specify the important relationships, not a list of independent body parts:

- **Line of action:** the dominant curve/diagonal through head, torso and
  pelvis.
  Allow a calm vertical when it serves the scene; do not tilt everything by
  habit.
- **Shoulders and pelvis:** their relative tilt/rotation determines the
  gesture.
  Counter-tilt can produce relaxed contrapposto; rotation can convey turning
  or recoil.
- **Support and weight:** identify the loaded foot, seated contact or
  supporting
  hand. Keep the mass supported or make the transient motion clear. Clothes,
  hair
  and loose accessories respond to gravity and movement.
- **Near/far limbs:** distinguish them through overlap, scale and contour
  breaks.
  Preserve an understandable torso-to-pelvis connection and joint directions.
- **Hands:** give each a purpose such as support, invitation, adjustment or
  emphasis.
  Avoid automatic collar-gripping and hand-on-hip on every image. Check wrist
  angle,
  thumb placement, fingers, object contact and plausible hidden portions.
- **Silhouette and negative spaces:** separate important limbs from the torso;
  deliberate overlap is fine when the gesture remains readable.

For a new pose, keep the face/outfit canon but release the old body's
arrangement.
Do not ask the generator to preserve the original pose while simultaneously
requesting a new action. Clothing drape and garment folds must be allowed to
adapt.

## Camera is part of acting

Choose viewpoint, framing and perspective for the emotional relationship:

- **Near eye-level:** connection, dialogue or parity. Gaze matches the
  intended
  partner, not the lens by default.
- **Slightly elevated close view:** playful approach, proximity or a curious
  gesture. Head, torso and limbs share the same perspective.
- **Low close view:** stature, attitude or dramatic foreground overlap. Do not
  replace body structure with arbitrary enlargement.
- **Seated or floor-level composition:** pauses, informal exchanges and strong
  near/far depth. Contact points, support and bent joints remain plausible.
- **Wider establishing CG:** shared action and environmental storytelling.
  Characters stay readable and belong to one space.

Wide-angle perspective changes near/far scale; a tilted frame changes
orientation.
They are different choices and neither automatically makes a pose expressive.
Use foreshortening deliberately: establish the near form, overlap and attached
limb before exaggerating it. Keep the face readable unless its concealment is
the intended story point. Avoid accidental joint crops and tangencies.

For adult sensual fashion, let posture, fabric tension/drape, gaze and camera
create the requested attraction. Maintain clearly adult identity and
intentional
coverage. A low or close camera is not permission for unrequested intimate
exposure.

## Facial acting and attention

Coordinate brows, lids, eye direction, cheeks and mouth. A wink, crooked grin,
pressed lips or open laugh should fit the action, not simply add more
expression
markers. Slight facial asymmetry often communicates character better than a
perfectly mirrored expression. A frightened face, embarrassed smile and
teasing
smirk must not collapse into the same half-lidded look.

Let gaze lead to the important relationship: an object in hand, a companion,
the player or something beyond frame. Body and face can intentionally
contrast,
but the contrast should tell a story rather than look anatomically
disconnected.
Use hair and accessories as secondary motion, not noise across the eyes.

## Assign each pose reference a role

Use the [pose reference map](../../../../assets/visual-project-skills/ink-punk/references/pose-reference-map.md) to choose what to study.
Inspect the relevant reference when it is available for the current task.
They govern geometry, gesture, camera or expression only. Preserve the
fictional
character's face, adult identity, proportions, clothes and graphic rendering.
Do not inherit background bystanders, text, watermark, uniform motifs or a
model's
likeness. If a literal transfer conflicts with the character's proportions or
wardrobe, preserve the action and adapt the mechanics.

For a set of CGs, vary the scene beat, support, orientation, framing and gaze.
Do not reuse one pose with different heads and palettes. Preserve a quiet
scene
when requested; every CG need not be acrobatic or wide-angle.

## A useful pose prompt

> Keep [fictional identity and outfit] from image A. Use image B only for
> [pose/weight/camera], image C only for [expression], and image D for
> rendering
> or environment if needed. Moment: [beat]. Body: [line of action, support,
> shoulder/pelvis relationship and purposeful hand gesture]. Camera:
> [viewpoint,
> crop and deliberate near/far relationship]. Gaze and face: [specific
> acting].
> Allow folds and hair to respond to the new action. Preserve adult
> proportions,
> garment identity and the Ink Punk drawing language. Exclude [unwanted
> transfer].

Supply actual selected images within the active tool's limits. Do not load all
photos or stack conflicting poses into a single request. For photographic pose
references, state explicitly that likeness and photo styling are not targets.

## Gallery acceptance

Inspect the actual artwork, not just the prompt:

Start with a whole-figure branch inventory before checking individual joints.
Count all arm/leg-shaped contours, including dark sleeves between two otherwise
plausible arms. Give each branch one shoulder/hip and one intended continuation;
do not excuse an extra cylinder as a fold or add a hand to it. Natural occlusion
is allowed when the hidden continuation remains plausible.

Read clothing as a connected object. The collar and back yoke, shoulder seams,
armholes, sleeves and cuffs must agree on how it is worn. For example, a shirt
securely on both shoulders cannot simultaneously have its same shoulder section
fallen to the upper arm while the collar remains suspended at the neck. An
intentional one-sided slip is possible if the entire garment drapes accordingly;
do not turn this repair into a universal ban on asymmetry or off-shoulder fashion.
When a garment correction changes its construction, check its paired sprite/CG
as well, preserving face, coverage and successful acting.

For visible arms, follow the whole shoulder-to-upper-arm-to-elbow-to-forearm-
to-wrist-to-palm chain. Locate the elbow under the garment and compare plausible
segment lengths and bend direction, allowing for perspective. Check that the
palm actually supports weight or contacts its object. Sleeve folds are not
evidence of a sound joint, and a good-looking hand does not validate its arm.
State uncertainty when anatomy is obscured rather than declaring it correct.
Also trace each leg hip–thigh–knee–shin–ankle–foot and reconcile the torso/pelvis
twist with both legs and the loaded contact. Loose fabric is not proof of a
coherent skeleton. If the connection reads broken or cannot be made convincing,
correct it before marking the image ready; natural purposeful occlusion alone
is not a reason to force every limb into view.

Check tools and held objects independently of hand anatomy: the working tip or
jaw must meet the right surface with believable overlap and force direction.
A tool drawn on top of a lens, or a wrench ambiguously layered around a bolt,
can fail even when its fingers look pleasant.

Check fixtures as functional objects, beyond hand contact. For a pool ladder,
trace both deck anchors into continuous rails, the two in-water stiles and the
treads joining them; reconcile the dry deck, coping, wall and water plane. A
floating far rail is still broken even when the near rail and both hands look
good. For another object, identify its actual mounting and working relationship
rather than imposing this ladder recipe. Use a real construction reference or
clarify the camera if the attachment cannot be read; more metallic highlights
do not repair it.

Verify the requested action independently of allure: entering water requires an
actual descent with support and attention to the next step, rather than a poolside
fashion pose. Gaze at the steps may serve that moment better than eye contact.
Keep sexuality in fit, silhouette and body rhythm without forcing viewer flirtation
against the action.

Also check the requested world against the actual environment. A style anchor
can supply ink, cel shadows and lighting without supplying its city type or
landmarks. An ordinary real-world night city needs plausible building masses,
roofs and window grids; fantasy towers/bridges belong only when requested.

- Can the intended beat be described from body, face and composition?
- Is weight/support believable, including any unseen continuation outside
  frame?
- Are torso, pelvis, limbs, hands and perspective structurally consistent?
- Is the focal gesture readable at the actual gallery thumbnail size?
- Are identity, costume and Ink Punk rendering maintained while the pose
  changes?
- Does lighting and physical contact tie the actor into the environment?
- Do cropping and any required title/UI safe areas match actual project needs?

Use the project's export/aspect/thumbnail requirements when known; do not
invent
universal resolution or safe-area numbers. Inspect relevant crops before
calling
them gallery-ready. A generated CG is an illustration; saving, unlocking and
displaying it in a player's gallery require separate implementation and
testing.

Correct the structural or acting problem specifically. More costume detail,
larger anatomy or stronger lighting does not repair a broken gesture. Mark a
new demonstration as provisional until the user adopts it as a reference.
